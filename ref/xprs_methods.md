# XPRS Methods Note

## Citation
Kim N, Lee S. *XPRS: a tool for interpretable and explainable polygenic risk score*. Bioinformatics. 2025;41(4):btaf143. doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btaf143.

## Source Scope
This note is a curated methodology reference derived from the cited open-access XPRS paper. It is intended to give the portal a reliable, human-readable source for answering questions about how XPRS works.

## What XPRS Is
XPRS is an interpretation and visualization framework for polygenic risk scores (PRSs). Its purpose is not to replace a PRS model, but to make an existing PRS more interpretable at the level of genomic regions, genes, and individual SNPs.

## Core Idea
The paper describes XPRS as decomposing a PRS into:
- gene/region contribution scores
- SNP contribution scores

The decomposition is performed using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). In the paper, the gene-level attributed value is explicitly described as equivalent to the SHAP value in a linear model.

## Inputs
XPRS requires three mandatory input files:
- a genotype file in binary PLINK format
- a PRS scoring file
- a GWAS association file

More specifically:
- The PRS scoring file contains SNPs, SNP IDs, effect alleles, and beta coefficients, and can be obtained from sources such as the PGS Catalog.
- The paper recommends using PRS scoring files generated by LD-aware PRS methods to reduce inflation from linkage disequilibrium.
- The GWAS association file is derived from the GWAS Catalog and contains genes mapped to significant variants reported in prior GWAS studies.
- If a cohort genotype file is used, individuals should belong to the same ancestry to avoid population stratification bias.
- If an individual genotype file is used, the reference genotype file should match that individual's ancestry so SNP effect size estimation is appropriate.

## How XPRS Is Calculated
The paper describes XPRS as having three main processes:

1. Preprocessing and variant mapping
2. Calculation of contribution scores and attributed values
3. Visualization

In plain language, XPRS explains *why* an individual's PRS is high or low by identifying which genes, regions, and SNPs are contributing most strongly to that score.

## Step 1: Preprocessing and Variant Mapping
The preprocessing stage:
- aligns SNPs across the data sources
- standardizes the PRS
- adjusts beta coefficients accordingly
- maps SNPs to corresponding genes

The SNP-to-gene mapping follows a sequential protocol:

1. Positional mapping
   - SNPs are assigned to nearby genes by genomic proximity
   - the default window is 200 kb
   - the window can be adjusted by the user

2. Combined SNP-to-Gene (cS2G) mapping
   - integrates functional genomics evidence such as eQTL and Hi-C information
   - allows SNPs to map to genes not captured by proximity alone

3. GWAS P-value- or SNP heritability-based mapping
   - used to assign remaining SNPs to meaningful loci

At this stage, one SNP may map to multiple genes because of both the positional window and functional mapping evidence.

The paper also applies a `regionizing` step:
- genes with overlapping SNP profiles are consolidated into distinct regions based on shared SNP content
- this is intended to improve computational efficiency and focus attention on meaningful risk loci

## Step 2: Calculation of Scores
After mapping, XPRS computes:
- the polygenic risk score (PRS)
- gene contribution score (`CSgene`)
- variance of the gene contribution score
- attributed value of the gene (`Agene`)
- SNP contribution score (`CSSNP`)

## Gene-Level Contribution
The paper gives an explicit definition for the gene contribution score. It quantifies how much each gene contributes to increasing the PRS of an individual:

$$CS_{\mathrm{gene},j} = \sum_{i \in j} \left(\beta_{\mathrm{std},i} \cdot G_{0,i}\right)$$

where:
- $\beta_{\mathrm{std},i}$ is the standardized effect size of SNP $i$
- $G_i$ is the individual's genotype for SNP $i$
- $p_i$ is the allele frequency of SNP $i$
- $G_{0,i} = G_i - p_i$

The paper explains that because adjusted beta coefficients are used, the genotype is recalculated by subtracting the allele frequency.

Conceptually:
- `CSgene` is the per-gene contribution score
- it measures how much a gene contributes to the overall PRS
- higher absolute contribution means greater effect on the individual's genetic risk profile

The current portal's `Individual Contribution` field corresponds closely to this gene-level contribution concept.

## Population Rank
The paper does not use the exact UI label `Population Rank`, but it does define population-based comparison quantities that explain the portal behavior.

Two pieces are especially important:
- the variance of `CSgene` across the population
- the gene attributed value `Agene`

The variance of `CSgene_j` across individuals is used to identify genes that contribute most strongly to PRS variation in the population. The paper states that this variance is equivalent to the heritability due to gene `j`.

The attributed value is defined as:

$$A_{\mathrm{gene},j} = CS_{\mathrm{gene},j} - \operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{population}}\left(CS_{\mathrm{gene},j}\right)$$

This quantity identifies genes whose contribution in an individual is above or below the population average. The paper explicitly says this is the SHAP value in a linear model.

In practical portal terms:
- `Individual Contribution` reflects the individual's own gene-level contribution
- `Population Rank` is a comparative contextualization view
- the paper's formal population comparison quantities are variance of `CSgene` and `Agene`

## SNP-Level Contribution
The SNP contribution score is defined as:

$$CS_{\mathrm{SNP},i} = \beta_{\mathrm{std},i} \cdot G_{0,i}$$

This is the per-variant contribution of SNP `i` to the individual's PRS. The paper frames this as the SNP-level decomposition that supports LocusZoom-like visualization and fine-grained explanation within a gene or region.

## Visualizations and Outputs
The paper describes a multilevel visualization strategy including:
- Manhattan plots
- LocusZoom-like plots
- tables at the population and individual levels
- density plots for individual PRS context

Population-level output:
- Manhattan plots and tables identify important genes based on the highest variance of `CSgene`
- genes are ranked by variance rather than thresholded
- the top 10 genes with the highest variance are highlighted in red

Individual-level output:
- density plots show where an individual's overall PRS sits relative to the reference population
- gene-based Manhattan plots show which genes or regions drive high or low PRS values
- LocusZoom-like plots show SNP-level contributions within a selected gene or region
- the top 10 genes with the highest contribution are highlighted in red and the bottom 10 with the lowest contribution are highlighted in blue

Within the portal, these ideas are reflected by:
- the individual Manhattan plot
- the PRS density plot
- the top-gene summary table
- gene-level narrative interpretation

## Intended Use
XPRS is designed to make PRS outputs easier to interpret for downstream users, including clinicians and patients. The paper frames this as a way to bridge complex genetic risk information with actionable, explainable output.

The paper repeatedly emphasizes communication benefits:
- helping users understand which genes and SNPs drive risk
- increasing trust in PRS-based systems
- supporting communication between clinicians and patients

## Important Limits
- XPRS is an interpretability layer on top of a PRS, not a standalone risk model.
- It assumes a linear PRS framework in the way contribution scores and SHAP-style interpretations are described.
- Proper ancestry matching matters for both cohort and individual analyses.
- SNP-to-gene mapping is heuristic and multi-stage, so one SNP may map to multiple genes.
- The portal UI labels are not always identical to the paper's variable names, so `Individual Contribution` and `Population Rank` should be interpreted in light of `CSgene`, variance of `CSgene`, and `Agene`.

## Evidence and Example Results From the Paper
- For the population-level demonstration, the paper used 503 East Asian samples from the 1000 Genomes Project and a type 2 diabetes PRS.
- The paper reports that KCNQ1 and KCNQ1OT1 showed the greatest variance in gene contribution score in the type 2 diabetes example.
- For the individual-level demonstration, the paper uses example individuals HG00464 and HG01816 from the 1000 Genomes Project.
- In HG00464, the PRS was described as being in the top 2% of the population.
- The paper uses examples such as CDKAL1 and KCNQ1 to show how specific genes can elevate or reduce an individual's PRS.

## High-Confidence Facts From the Paper
- XPRS is an explainable/interpretable PRS tool.
- It decomposes PRSs into genes/regions and SNP contribution scores.
- The decomposition uses SHAP.
- It requires three mandatory input files: genotype, PRS scoring, and GWAS association files.
- It uses sequential SNP-to-gene mapping plus a regionizing step.
- It computes `CSgene`, variance of `CSgene`, `Agene`, and `CSSNP`.
- It supports multilevel visualization including Manhattan plots, LocusZoom-like plots, density plots, and tables.
- It is implemented as a user-facing web interface.
- The software is available on GitHub at `https://github.com/nayeonkim93/XPRS`.
- The demo/web service is available at `https://xprs.leelabsg.org/`.
