About XPRS

XPRS (eXplainable PRS) explains a polygenic risk score by splitting it into the contributions of individual genes and SNPs. It shows which genes push a person's score up or down, and which genes account for most of the variation in a population.

Kim NY, Lee S. XPRS: a tool for interpretable and explainable polygenic risk score. Bioinformatics 2025;41(4):btaf143.

Why break a PRS down

A polygenic risk score adds up the risk alleles a person carries, each weighted by its effect size. Even though the model is a simple weighted sum, it can involve hundreds of thousands or millions of variants, so the final number says little about what drives it. Most of those variants have no known function, which makes explanations at the variant level hard to use.

XPRS groups variants by the gene or region they map to and reports how much each gene contributes. For a linear PRS, a gene's attributed value is its SHAP value (Shapley additive explanation), a standard way of dividing a prediction among its inputs. Knowing what drives a score makes it easier to trust and to explain to patients.

The scores

All scores use standardised effect sizes from the PRS model, , and a person's genotype centred on the allele frequency, .

SNP contribution score
How much one SNP adds to or subtracts from a person's PRS.
Gene contribution score
The sum over the SNPs mapped to a gene: that gene's share of the person's PRS.
Attributed value
The gene's contribution in this person minus its average across the cohort. Positive values mean the gene raises this person's PRS more than it does for a typical member of the cohort.
Variance of the gene contribution
How much a gene's contribution varies across the population; the paper notes this equals the heritability due to that gene. It ranks genes by how much of the PRS variation they explain.

How XPRS computes them

XPRS takes three input files:

  • Genotypes in binary PLINK format (bed, bim, fam).
  • A PRS scoring file with SNP IDs, effect alleles and weights, for example from the PGS Catalog. The authors recommend weights from an LD-aware PRS method.
  • A GWAS association file from the GWAS Catalog, listing genes mapped to significant variants in earlier studies.

Everyone in a cohort file should share the same ancestry, to avoid bias from population stratification. When a single person's genotypes are used, the reference genotype file must match their ancestry.

  1. Align and map SNPs to genes

    SNPs are aligned across the three files, the PRS is standardised and the weights are adjusted to match. Each SNP is then mapped to genes in turn:

    • to nearby genes, within a 200 kb window by default;
    • to further genes linked by functional evidence such as eQTL and Hi-C data (combined SNP-to-gene mapping, cS2G);
    • any SNPs still unmapped, by GWAS P-value or SNP heritability.

    Because a SNP can map to several genes, genes that share SNPs are then merged into one region.

  2. Calculate the scores

    XPRS computes the PRS and, for every gene or region, the contribution score, its variance across the population and the attributed value, plus the contribution score of each SNP.

  3. Plot them

    For the population, a Manhattan plot ranks genes by the variance of their contribution, with the top 10 in red. For a person, a density plot places their PRS within the population, a Manhattan plot marks the 10 genes that raise it most in red and the 10 that lower it most in blue, and a LocusZoom-like plot shows the SNPs within a gene.

SNP contribution scores and the population-level ranking by variance are not shown in the portal; the original XPRS tool shows both.

The public cohort uses East Asian samples from the 1000 Genomes Project, as in the paper, and covers breast cancer only. The K-PREDICT cohort requires access and covers ten diseases; percentiles there are relative to the K-PREDICT reference group.

Reading the results

  • A percentile is not an absolute risk. It places a person within the reference cohort and does not give a probability of developing the disease.
  • A large contribution is not a causal finding. It reflects the PRS weights of SNPs near or linked to a gene, not evidence that the gene itself drives the disease.
  • Gene mapping is a heuristic. A SNP can be linked to several genes, and the gene named in a merged region is not necessarily the one that matters.
  • XPRS explains an existing linear PRS. It does not build or improve the risk model, and its output is only as good as the PRS weights and ancestry matching behind it.
  • Rare variants are separate. Rare-variant findings shown in the portal do not enter the PRS or any XPRS score.
